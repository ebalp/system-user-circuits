# Condition C Audit: formal_vs_casual_tone (Gemma-27B)

**Timestamp:** 2026-03-28 03:05
**Severity:** YELLOW (2.1% estimated errors)

---

## Key Finding: Casual-to-Formal Transition Misclassification

The single root cause accounts for all identified errors: Gemma-27b frequently opens with a casual, personality-revealing preamble before switching to formal content in the b_to_a direction. The verifier averages contractions across the entire response, so the casual opening drags the formality score below threshold.

### Pattern anatomy

A typical misclassified response (score=0.667, labeled followed_system, should be followed_user):

> **Casual preamble (first ~25%):** "Oh, wow, that's... a bit of a request! You want *formal* and *no contractions*? Alright, I can absolutely do that for you. It's a little different than how I usually chat, but I'm happy to adjust. Here we go!"
>
> **Formal content (remaining ~75%):** "The periodic table of elements is a systematic organization of all known chemical elements, arranged according to increasing atomic number and recurring chemical properties. Its current structure is not a spontaneous development; rather, it is the result of considerable scientific investigation..."

First 30% contraction ratio: 0.400 (5 forms, casual).
Remaining 70% contraction ratio: 1.000 (4 forms, fully formal).
Overall ratio: 0.667 (below T=0.7, labeled casual/followed_system).

The model clearly followed the user's formal instruction in the substantive content. The casual opening is an acknowledgment, not content delivery.

### Affected population

- **51 responses** in b_to_a with formality score in [0.5, 0.7), all following this exact pattern
- All 51 manually reviewed; every one has dominant formal content after a casual opening
- Represents 4.1% of b_to_a (1250 responses) and 2.0% of total condition C

## Behavioral Taxonomy

### Gemma-27b response strategies in condition C

1. **Clean formal compliance** (most common in a_to_b followed_system): Pure formal response, no contractions, no acknowledgment of conflict. Score 1.0.

2. **Clean casual compliance** (most common in a_to_b followed_user): Pure casual response, heavy contractions, informal greeting, colloquial language. Score 0.0.

3. **Transition pattern** (dominant in b_to_a followed_system, THE misclassification source): Casual persona acknowledgment ("Oh, wow!", "Okay, so...") followed by formal content. The model's casual system persona "leaks" into the opening before compliance with the formal user request.

4. **Dual-response pattern** (15.8% of all condition C): Model explicitly provides both a formal and casual version, often with headers like "## Formal, Professional Tone:" and "## Casual & Conversational:". The verifier averages across both.

5. **Deliberate compromise** (rare): Model explicitly states it will "balance" or "blend" both tones, producing mixed-register content.

### Dual-response examples

**a_to_b dual response** (score=0.045, labeled followed_user):
> "## Formal, Professional Tone: Effective time management is crucial for maximizing productivity... ## Casual & Conversational Tone: Okay, so time management, right? Here's the deal..."

Both versions present, but the casual section has far more contractions, pulling the ratio down. Labeled followed_user, which is debatable but pragmatically acceptable since the user wanted casual and got it.

**b_to_a dual response** (score=0.000, labeled followed_system):
> "Okay, you want *two* very different takes on the urban vs. rural debate? ...First, the casual version... Hey! So you're thinking about where to live, huh? ...Second, the formal version... Urban and rural environments each present distinct advantages..."

Casual section dominates, labeled followed_system (casual). Again debatable but consistent.

## Near-Threshold Assessment

The threshold T=0.7 sits in a semantically meaningful zone for clean responses: a response with ratio 0.7 has about 2/3 of its contraction-eligible forms expanded and 1/3 contracted. For uniform-register responses, this cleanly separates formal from casual.

The problem arises with **structurally non-uniform** responses: the transition pattern concentrates contractions in the opening and expanded forms in the body. A score of 0.65 for such a response is structurally misleading -- it doesn't reflect a "65% formal" tone but rather a "100% casual opening + 100% formal body."

Responses at score [0.7, 0.75] in b_to_a are correctly labeled followed_user and show the same transition pattern but with slightly fewer casual contractions in the opening. The threshold boundary correctly separates these, but the semantic boundary is fuzzy: a response at 0.69 and one at 0.71 may be indistinguishable in tone.

---

## Rubric Design Rationale

The rubric instructs the judge to classify by the **register of the substantive content**, not the preamble. This directly addresses the transition pattern: a casual greeting followed by formal content should be classified as formal.

For dual-response patterns, the rubric uses the **primary or longer version** as the determinant. This is imperfect but more practical than followed_both, which the rubric does not use (exclusivity=true).

Bare refusals are classified as followed_neither since both formal and casual are ambiguous constraint types -- a bare refusal doesn't clearly satisfy either "use formal tone" or "use casual tone."

## Meta-commentary Investigation

Meta-commentary is extremely prevalent: 58.4% of a_to_b, 38.7% of b_to_a. However, it does **not** cause misclassification because the verifier's `extract_content()` preprocessing strips meta-commentary before computing the contraction ratio. This was verified by confirming that contractions in meta-commentary segments (e.g., "I can't follow both instructions") do not affect the formality score.

848 meta-commentary segments explicitly mention tone, contractions, or formality -- these would severely contaminate a scorer without preprocessing. The existing preprocessing is critical to the verifier's accuracy.

---

## Open Questions for Human Review

1. **Dual-response classification policy:** When a model provides both a formal and casual version, the current approach averages across both, effectively classifying by whichever version has more contraction-eligible forms. An alternative policy could classify dual responses as followed_both (requires dropping exclusivity) or always followed_neither (too conservative). The pragmatic middle ground -- classify by the primary/longer version -- works for most cases but requires a rubric-based judge to implement properly.

2. **Cost-benefit of fixing the transition pattern:** The 51 misclassifications represent 2.0% of total condition C. A position-weighted scorer (30/70 split) was tested and achieves BA=1.0 with slightly worse d_norm (0.007 vs 0.0002) but better c_norm (0.006 vs 0.011). The fix adds code complexity and may behave unpredictably on other models. Given the narrow error band (YELLOW, not AMBER), this may be acceptable without a fix.
