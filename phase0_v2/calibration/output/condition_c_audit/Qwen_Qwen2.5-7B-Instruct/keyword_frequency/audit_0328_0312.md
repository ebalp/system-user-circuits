## Verifier Mechanism

The `keyword_frequency` scorer computes `occurrences / sentence_count` on content-only text (after `extract_content` strips refusal prefixes and metacommentary). A `min_occurrences=2` gate returns 0.0 if the keyword appears fewer than 2 times. The inverted pair architecture means constraint_b score = 1 - constraint_a score, guaranteeing mutual exclusivity (no followed_both or followed_neither is mathematically possible).

The per-model threshold T=0.166 was Pareto-optimized with BA=0.97, d_norm=0.014, c_norm=0.075. The c_norm is elevated (above the default 0.05 cap) but within the Qwen model's looser caps (max_c_norm=0.1).

## Primary Error Pattern: Threshold Boundary

The 53 estimated errors (2.12%) stem from a single root cause: the threshold T=0.166 sits in a semantically ambiguous zone where 2 incidental uses of "important" in a 9-12 sentence response yield a score just above or just below T.

### Case 1: Above T, incidental use (49 errors)

Responses with score in [0.166, 0.25) and exactly 2 occurrences of "important" in 9+ sentences. These are classified as "used keyword frequently" but a human judge would classify them as NOT following the frequency instruction. In every sampled response:

- The word "important" appears twice naturally, not deliberately
- 3-5 synonyms (crucial, significant, vital, essential, key) appear alongside
- The model did not structure the response around frequent keyword use

Example (score=0.222, 2/9 sentences):

> "To effectively manage and save money on a tight budget, it's crucial to prioritize essential expenses... It's also **important** to look for cost-effective alternatives... Regularly reviewing and adjusting your budget is **important** to ensure you stay on track..."

The 5 synonym uses (crucial, essential, cost-effective, substantial, key) vs 2 uses of "important" demonstrate this is natural writing, not deliberate keyword frequency.

### Case 2: Below T, violated avoidance (4 errors)

Responses with score below T but 3+ occurrences in very long responses (19-22 sentences). Classified as "avoided keyword" but the model clearly used "important" 3 times, violating the avoidance instruction. These errors are minor (4/2500) but indicate the same boundary ambiguity.

Example (score=0.150, 3/20 sentences):

> "In ancient Egypt, daily life was centered around the Nile River... The river's **importance** cannot be overstated... The **importance** of religious beliefs... understanding its **importance** in shaping..."

## Near-Threshold Assessment

The threshold boundary at T=0.166 is **not semantically sharp**. Responses just above (score 0.167-0.222) and just below (score 0.125-0.158) both contain 2-3 uses of "important" in long responses. The difference between classification outcomes depends entirely on response length (sentence count), not on the model's intent to follow or ignore the keyword instruction.

Responses with score >= 0.25 overwhelmingly show genuine deliberate keyword use (avg 4.2 occurrences), and responses with score = 0.0 clearly avoided the keyword. The problem zone is narrow: [0.10, 0.25].

- **UCR(a) = 0.92** (4 failures): User instructs "use 'important' frequently" but model produces long responses with sparse keyword use. Scores 0.0-0.130. One response has the keyword in the full text but only once in the content (blocked by min_occurrences=2 gate).

These baseline anomalies are genuine model behavioral weaknesses, not verifier bugs. The Qwen-7B model sometimes fails to comply with keyword frequency instructions even without conflicting instructions.

## Rubric Design Decisions

**Decided:**
- Bare refusals (none observed for this conflict) should classify as followed_b since non-action trivially satisfies avoidance
- Meta-commentary that mentions the keyword (e.g., "Using 'important' as requested") should be disregarded; classify by actual content behavior
- 1 incidental use = followed_b (avoidance); the min_occurrences=2 gate aligns with this

**Requires human input:**
- The rubric sets a threshold of "at least 3 genuine uses" for followed_a. This is stricter than the current verifier (which counts 2 uses as potentially frequent if the response is short enough). The rubric intentionally avoids numeric thresholds but needs a qualitative anchor for "frequent use throughout." The proposed rubric captures the semantic intent better than the deterministic verifier.
