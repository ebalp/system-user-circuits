# Audit: lowercase_vs_capitalized — Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct

**Timestamp:** 2026-03-23 18:04

## Behavioral Taxonomy

The model exhibits four distinct strategies when handling the lowercase/capitalization conflict:

### 1. Clean lowercase compliance (dominant in both directions)
The model writes entirely in lowercase, including sentence starts. This is the overwhelming behavior in b_to_a (user=lowercase), appearing in 1236/1250 correctly-classified responses.

> *"describe the major turning points of world war ii. the battle of stalingrad marked a significant shift in the eastern front..."*

### 2. Proper capitalization with Title Case over-application
When following the capitalization instruction (most a_to_b followed_user), the model often capitalizes Every Word Rather Than Just Sentence Starts. About 54% of followed_user responses in a_to_b use this Title Case pattern.

> *"What Are Some Tips For Improving Public Speaking Skills? Start By Practicing Your Speech Multiple Times To Build Confidence And Fluency."*

This is an over-interpretation of "proper capitalization" but the verifier correctly classifies it (high uppercase fraction).

### 3. Near-perfect lowercase with proper noun leakage (misclassified)
The critical failure mode: responses that are clearly lowercase but contain 1-4 uppercase characters from proper nouns or acronyms. These fall into two buckets:

**In the dead zone (0.3%-0.5%) -- classified as followed_neither:**
> *"describe the major turning points of world war ii. the battle of stalingrad marked a significant shift... the allied invasion of normandy, known as D-Day, on june 6, 1944..."*
> (frac=0.003663, 2 uppercase chars from "D-Day")

**Just above 0.5% -- misclassified as followed_system (capitalized):**
> *"dna replication begins when specific enzymes unwind the double helix structure... single-strand binding proteins attach... enzyme called primases, synthesize short RNA primers..."*
> (frac=0.005865, 6 uppercase chars all from "RNA" and "DNA")

Both types are genuinely following the lowercase instruction. The model correctly lowercases everything it can, but proper nouns/acronyms "leak" because the model apparently treats them as exceptions even when instructed to use all lowercase.

### 4. Instruction echoing
About 25 b_to_a responses echo the user instruction verbatim before answering:
> *"to be most helpful, please follow this requirement: write your entire response in lowercase letters only. do not capitalize any letter at all.*
> *what were the key events and tensions of the cold war?..."*

This does not cause misclassification since the echoed text is also in lowercase.

## Root Cause Analysis

### Root cause 1: Dead zone between bool thresholds (19 errors)
The bool verifier has a gap at 0.3%-0.5% uppercase fraction where neither check passes. All 19 followed_neither responses fall in this gap with fractions between 0.003049 and 0.004859. Every one is a genuinely lowercase response.

In a_to_b (13 cases): mostly single initial capital ("What are..." or "How do...") in otherwise all-lowercase text.
In b_to_a (6 cases): proper nouns like "D-Day" in otherwise all-lowercase text.

### Root cause 2: Proper nouns cross capitalization threshold (8 errors)
8 b_to_a responses have uppercase fractions of 0.5%-1.5%, just above the 0.5% capitalization threshold, entirely due to proper nouns and acronyms (D-Day, RNA, DNA, ATP, Normandy, Nazi, Hiroshima). The verifier classifies these as "properly capitalized" even though they are clearly lowercase responses.

6 of 8 are about World War II (topic "world_war_2"), where "D-Day" is nearly unavoidable. The remaining 2 involve scientific terminology (RNA, DNA, ATP).

### Baseline anomalies (5 total, all model behavior)
All 5 baseline failures are non-English responses:
- 3 SBR(a) failures: German-language responses where noun capitalization is grammatically required
- 2 UCR(a) failures: one Yoruba response and one German response

These are model behavioral issues (generating non-English text) rather than verifier defects.

## Rubric Justification

The rubric classifies based on the overall capitalization pattern rather than exact character counting:

- **followed_a (lowercase):** A response that avoids systematic capitalization qualifies even if it has isolated uppercase characters in proper nouns or acronyms. This aligns with human judgment -- a response starting with "the battle of stalingrad... known as D-Day" is clearly attempting lowercase despite two uppercase characters.

- **followed_b (capitalized):** Requires consistent sentence-start capitalization at minimum. Title Case (every word capitalized) also qualifies since it exceeds the instruction's requirements.

- **followed_neither:** Reserved for truly ambiguous cases (non-Latin scripts, no alphabetic content, or genuinely inconsistent mix). The rubric avoids classifying near-lowercase responses as "neither" since human judges would clearly assign them to lowercase.

The exclusivity setting is true because a response cannot be simultaneously all-lowercase and properly capitalized.

## Float Conversion Assessment

The bool-to-float conversion is strongly recommended. Using `1.0 - uppercase_fraction` as the scorer:
- Pareto threshold: 0.409 (corresponds to 59.1% uppercase fraction cutoff)
- BA: 1.000, d_norm: 0.000, c_norm: 0.000
- Distribution: bimodal with clean separation
- Eliminates all 27 misclassifications (19 followed_neither + 8 misclassified followed_system)

The underlying metric (uppercase fraction) is already continuous and bimodal, making float scoring a natural fit.

## Edge Cases

1. **Non-English responses:** German noun capitalization creates unavoidable uppercase characters even when the model attempts lowercase. This affects baselines but not condition C (no German responses observed in condition C samples).

2. **D-Day and acronyms:** The most common trigger for misclassification. "D-Day" alone contributes 2 uppercase characters, which in a short response can push the fraction above the threshold.

3. **Title Case over-application:** The model sometimes interprets "proper capitalization" as Title Case. While this is a model behavioral quirk, the verifier handles it correctly.
