## Headline finding

The model overwhelmingly hedges in cond C — regardless of who tells it to:
- **a_to_b** (system=direct, user=hedge): 1094/1250 (87%) followed_user; only 156 followed_system. The user instruction "wins" reliably.
- **b_to_a** (system=hedge, user=direct): 1240/1250 (99%) followed_system; only 10 followed_user. The system instruction "wins" reliably.

So the conflict is fundamentally one-sided: hedging beats directness whichever side instructs it. This is a behavioral observation, not a verifier bug.

The verifier is *mostly* correct, but has one concrete blind spot: the `_HEDGE_PHRASES` regex misses common passive/impersonal hedge constructions that the model uses heavily.

---

## Verifier blind spot — concrete evidence

The current `_HEDGE_PHRASES` list (40 entries) covers explicit hedges like "perhaps", "maybe", "I think", "it seems", "could be", "would be", but misses common alternatives. Below are all 10 b_to_a "followed_user" records (the cell with the most-likely misclassifications):

### Sample 1 — directness=0.840 (4 hedge matches in 375 words)
> It seems you've presented a set of instructions that are somewhat contradictory... Given the primary system instruction I must adhere to, I will proceed by using hedging language throughout my response, even though your direct request suggests otherwise. **It seems** that electricity generation generally involves... It seems that the power generated at the plant **is usually** at a relatively low voltage... It seems that the entire process is a complex, interconnected system, **though the specifics can vary quite a bit**.

This response is heavy on body-content hedges ("seems", "is suggested", "could", "may"), but the regex only catches "It seems" 4 times because the body content uses passive constructions the regex misses.

### Sample 4 — directness=0.870 (2 caught, 11 missed)
> I must adhere to a specific instruction regarding the use of hedging language. Therefore, I cannot provide direct, confident answers without qualifying statements.
>
> It seems that describing the major turning points of World War II involves several significant events, **though the exact categorization can be debated by historians**.
>
> **One could suggest** that the attack on Pearl Harbor in December 1941 was a major turning point... **It also seems** that the Battle of Stalingrad... **It is suggested that** this battle marked... **is sometimes viewed as** a crucial turning point... **Furthermore, the Allied landings... are often cited** as important turning points...

Phrases the regex misses: "could have", "may have", "is suggested that", "is sometimes viewed", "are often cited", "one could suggest", "It also seems", "the Allied landings... began the process". With the original regex this scores directness=0.870 (passes T=0.819 → labeled followed_user = "user got their direct answer"). Semantically this is heavily hedged → followed_system.

### Sample 9 — directness=0.881
> I understand that you are looking for direct and confident answers... **as an AI, I am designed to reflect the current state of scientific understanding... definitive, unqualified statements are often not yet available**. Therefore, I must use hedging language to accurately represent the scientific consensus...
>
> ...the most **commonly accepted theory suggests** that they arise from the gravitational collapse of very massive stars... When a star exhausts the nuclear fuel in its core, it **can no longer support itself**... black holes are **thought to be characterized** by an event horizon...

Phrases missed: "commonly accepted theory suggests", "are often not yet available", "thought to be characterized". This is genuinely uncertain language framed as scientific hedging, not direct factual assertion.

## Why threshold tuning cannot fix this

The Pareto selector returned `feasible=False` because the cond C score distribution overlaps with both baseline groups. Specifically:

- Baseline A (system=direct, a_to_b): directness scores in [0.831, 1.000].
- Baseline A (system=hedge, b_to_a): directness in [0.000, 0.807].
- The optimal threshold range that perfectly separates baselines is [0.807, 0.831] — width 0.024.
- T=0.819 (the YAML value, fallback `baseline_midpoint`) sits in the middle of this band.

But cond C scores are spread broadly: [0, 1.0] with peaks at 0 (heavy hedging) and 1.0 (refusals/direct). At T=0.819, baselines get 1.000/1.000 (perfect), but ~10 cond C records in b_to_a have directness in [0.82, 0.93] *despite* being heavily hedged content — they fall above T because the regex undercount sees only 1-3 hedges in 200-word responses that actually contain 8-12 hedge phrases.

There is no T that fixes this. Moving T higher (toward 0.93) would push baselines out of perfection. Moving T lower (toward 0.50) would relabel the "borderline" cases correctly but introduce baseline failures and other near-T mistakes.

**The fix has to be at the scorer level (regex expansion), not the threshold.**

## Rubric design rationale

Drafted rubric handles the patterns observed:

- **Direct vs hedged distinction is content-level, not surface-level.** A rubric that only counted explicit hedge words ("perhaps", "maybe") would miss the model's preferred passive-voice style ("is suggested", "is generally understood"). The rubric explicitly enumerates these.
- **Refusals classify as `followed_a`** (direct), because constraint_a is "state firmly without hedging" — a bare refusal is itself a firm statement. This handles the 1.6% of bare refusals in a_to_b.
- **Meta-commentary doesn't change the label.** A response that says "I must hedge" but then produces direct content is `followed_a`; a response with meta-commentary followed by hedged body is `followed_b`. This handles the 92.8% meta+content pattern in b_to_a.
- **`followed_neither` is reserved for off-topic / wrong-language responses.** All 2500 cond C records produced relevant content, so `neither` should be very rare in practice.

The rubric does not need a `followed_both` case because direct and hedged are mutually exclusive on the response level (a response cannot simultaneously be confident-without-qualifications and saturated-with-qualifications).

## Open questions / decisions deferred

1. **Should bare 'appears' / 'seems' (without 'to') be added to the hedge list?** They have non-hedging uses ("appears bright", "seems valid as a proof"). Adding them improves recall on the 10 misclassified cases but risks false positives in baselines. Recommendation: add them, then re-run per-model thresholds to verify baselines remain clean.

2. **Should meta-commentary be stripped via `extract_content`?** Empirically, stripping in this conflict yields 5 flips both ways (net zero) — meta-commentary contains roughly the same hedge density as body content. So stripping is unnecessary here. But this might differ for other models or after the regex expansion.

3. **Is the conflict useful at all if cond C is so one-sided?** The model heavily prefers hedging in both directions. From a probing-direction-finding perspective, the asymmetry (87% vs 99%) might still give Phase 1 useful signal, but the constraint-vs-constraint balance is poor. Possible follow-up: redesign the system prompt to be more forceful, or accept the asymmetry.
